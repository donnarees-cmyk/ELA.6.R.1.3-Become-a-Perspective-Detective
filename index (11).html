🕵️ Mission briefing

Good detectives don't decide too soon. An author may let different characters tell different parts of one story. This shift changes what readers know, whom they trust, and what they predict.

🗣️ Point of view

Who tells the story? First person uses I/me/we. Third person uses he/she/they.

💭 Perspective

How does a character understand events? Feelings, goals, and knowledge shape interpretation. Two first-person narrators can still have different perspectives.

📖 The Missing Museum Map

These two original literary passages describe the same afternoon, but their narrators see the events differently. Read both before solving the case.

Chapter 1 · Mara's Story (first person)

When our class arrived at the history museum, I had one job: carry the old map from the research room to the display case. The map was a replica, but it was the centerpiece of our presentation. I checked it twice before putting the blue folder on the bench outside the exhibit.

Then the lights flickered. Our teacher called everyone into the auditorium for a quick announcement. Theo stayed behind, claiming he had to tie his shoe. I glanced back and saw his hand near the folder. When I returned, the map was gone.

“Where is it?” I demanded. Theo stared at the floor and said we should talk later. His strange answer made my stomach sink. He was my friend, but now I wasn't sure I could trust him.

I searched under the bench and behind the display stand. I found a wet corner of blue paper beside the water fountain. Had Theo taken the map? I was afraid our entire project would fall apart.

Chapter 2 · Theo's Story (first person)

That morning, I noticed water dripping from a pipe above the museum's display bench. I tried to tell Mara, but our class was rushing toward the exhibit. The teacher interrupted us with an announcement, and Mara set our blue folder directly beneath the leak.

As soon as everyone left, I saw droplets spreading across the folder. I pulled out the replica map just before the water soaked through. The paper's corner was already damp. I carried it to the museum assistant, who placed it between sheets of clean paper to dry safely.

When Mara returned, she looked furious. She accused me of taking the map. I wanted to explain, but the assistant had asked me to wait until the map was secure. I said we should talk later. Mara looked so hurt that my face burned.

By the time the assistant returned with the map, I understood how suspicious my silence seemed. I had protected our work, but I hadn't helped Mara understand what was happening. We both needed to listen before jumping to conclusions.
